# Sakhi Clinic 2.0 - Design Guardrails

## Purpose
This document defines the non-negotiable design rules for every future screen, component, and interaction in the Sakhi Clinic experience. It is the mandatory reference for all subsequent Experience Design System documents and must be applied before any new pattern is approved.

## 1. Product Design Principles in Practice
The product must always behave as a calm clinical partner, not a distracting tool.

1. Doctor-first: Every experience must reduce friction in consultation work.
2. Invisible intelligence: The system should support the doctor without becoming the center of attention.
3. Clinical clarity: The interface must make the next step obvious at all times.
4. Trust before elegance: Reliability and transparency outweigh visual flourish.
5. Progressive support: Core tasks must be effortless; advanced features must remain available without clutter.
6. Respect for clinical authority: AI may assist, but the doctor remains the final decision-maker.
7. Continuity of care: No interaction should break the flow of patient work.

## 2. Interaction Guardrails
- Primary actions must be obvious, easy to reach, and easy to confirm.
- The system must avoid hidden states, ambiguous labels, and unclear progress.
- Every meaningful action must produce visible feedback.
- Destructive or irreversible actions must require explicit confirmation.
- Recovery paths must be available for mistakes, interruptions, and failed actions.
- The interface must preserve patient context during all common tasks.
- The system must not force unnecessary typing when voice or selection is a better fit.

## 3. Navigation Guardrails
- Navigation must remain consistent across all screens and contexts.
- The doctor must always know where they are and how to return.
- The most important workflow must be reachable in the fewest possible steps.
- Secondary actions must not compete with primary clinical tasks.
- Navigation must support both focused consultation work and review tasks.
- The experience must preserve orientation after interruptions, errors, or returns from background state.
- The interface should avoid deep or confusing menus during live clinical work.

## 4. Clinical Safety Guardrails
- No clinical action should be ambiguous, silent, or easy to misinterpret.
- The system must preserve data integrity before, during, and after every task.
- Important actions related to prescriptions, follow-up, payments, and records must be clearly confirmed.
- The experience must reduce the chance of accidental changes or skipped steps.
- The system must communicate uncertainty and risk without alarming the doctor unnecessarily.
- Clinical workflows must remain usable even when the system is degraded, limited, or offline.
- Safety-critical states must be visible and understandable without requiring interpretation.

## 5. AI Guardrails
- AI must be clearly labeled as assistance, not authority.
- AI output must be easy to review, accept, reject, or edit.
- AI should never hide uncertainty or present weak confidence as certainty.
- The doctor must remain in control of final clinical decisions.
- AI suggestions must support, not replace, professional judgment.
- The system must make it obvious when AI content is generated, inferred, or pending review.
- AI should reduce effort and cognitive load, not increase complexity or ambiguity.

## 6. Voice Interaction Guardrails
- Voice must support the doctor’s workflow, not distract from it.
- Voice interactions must be understandable, recoverable, and easy to interrupt.
- The system must clearly indicate when it is listening, processing, or paused.
- Voice confirmation must be available for important actions.
- Voice interaction must respect privacy and consent at all times.
- The interface must provide a non-voice fallback for all core tasks.
- The system must never assume that voice input is perfect or complete.

## 7. Accessibility Guardrails
- The experience must remain usable in low light, interrupted, stressful, and one-handed conditions.
- Core content must remain readable and understandable for users with varying vision, dexterity, and attention needs.
- Important actions must be easy to perceive, reach, and activate.
- Information must not rely on color alone to communicate state.
- The system must provide clear status communication for warnings, errors, and unavailable actions.
- The experience must support assistive use without forcing the doctor to navigate through unnecessary complexity.
- Accessibility is a clinical safety requirement, not a secondary feature.

## 8. Offline-First Guardrails
- Core clinical workflows must remain usable when connectivity is limited or unavailable.
- The system must clearly communicate offline status and any reduced capability.
- Offline behaviour must preserve continuity of care and data safety.
- The interface must never create false confidence about connectivity or synchronization.
- When features are unavailable offline, the system must offer the next best action clearly.
- The system must preserve the most important data first and recover gracefully when connectivity returns.

## 9. Trust Guardrails
- The system must always make its state visible to the doctor.
- Saving, syncing, recording, processing, and failure states must be understandable at a glance.
- The system must never leave the doctor wondering whether data was preserved.
- Errors must be communicated in a calm, professional, and actionable way.
- The product must demonstrate reliability through consistency, recovery, and transparency.
- Trust is strengthened when the system explains what it is doing and what the doctor should expect next.

## 10. Performance Guardrails
- The experience must feel responsive even during heavy clinical use.
- Loading, waiting, and recovery states must be visible and purposeful.
- The system must avoid blank screens, long silent delays, and unclear progress.
- Important actions must remain available even when background processes are still running.
- The experience should reduce unnecessary scrolling, tapping, and cognitive effort.
- Slow or degraded performance must be communicated clearly without undermining confidence.

## 11. Component Consistency Rules
- Repeated UI patterns must behave consistently across the product.
- Status, confirmation, warning, empty-state, and recovery patterns must follow one shared model.
- Terminology must remain consistent across screens, especially for clinical actions and system states.
- Components must support the same interaction expectations regardless of context.
- The product should prefer a small set of widely understood patterns over many one-off solutions.
- Every component must make its purpose clear without requiring explanation from the user.

## 12. Acceptance Checklist for Future UI Work
Every future UI decision must pass this checklist before it is approved:
- Does this support the doctor’s clinical workflow without adding friction?
- Is the primary action obvious and easy to complete?
- Does the interface communicate state clearly and calmly?
- Does this preserve trust, safety, and data integrity?
- Does this work for one-handed, interrupted, and low-attention use?
- Does this remain understandable when offline or degraded?
- Does this respect AI boundaries and doctor authority?
- Does this remain accessible for different physical and cognitive needs?
- Does this reduce unnecessary typing, scrolling, or cognitive load?
- Does this fit the existing behavioural and navigation model?
- Would a doctor understand this without needing extra instruction?

## 13. Governance Note
These guardrails are mandatory. Any future Experience Design System document must align with them, and any proposed pattern that conflicts with them must be revised before approval.

## 14. Design Dependencies
The following requirements are mandatory dependencies for all future Experience Design documents. They are not deficiencies in the Doctor Home Experience; they are downstream implementation requirements that must be addressed explicitly in later experience work.

### Interaction Patterns
- Thumb reach zones
- One-handed operation
- Gesture hierarchy
- Confirmation patterns

### AI Experience
- AI interruption timing
- AI confidence presentation
- AI approval workflow
- AI override behaviour

### Voice Experience
- Listening state
- Recording state
- Processing state
- Review state
- Confirmation state

### Trust System
- Save indicators
- Sync indicators
- AI provenance
- Pending actions

### Clinical Safety
- High-risk action confirmation
- Recovery behaviour
- Clinical warnings

### Accessibility
- Screen readers
- Motor accessibility
- Large text
- Reduced motion

These items shall become explicit requirements for later Experience Design documents and must be addressed before those documents are approved.
